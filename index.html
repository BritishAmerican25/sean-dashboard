23-gate compliance · fail-closed

A high grade is necessary, not sufficient. Before any order, the candidate passes a 23-gate checklist logged to an audit trail — the same feed you see live in the dashboard. The gates encode the psychology mechanically: no impulse re-entry, no chasing, no fighting the tape.

The rule Sean leans on hardest: trade with the flow, never against it. A bullish setup with institutions quietly distributing into it is hard-blocked — the flow veto has caught more traps than any other single gate.

Where the idea is wrong

Every position has more than one door out. The premium stop is the catastrophic backstop; the real swing stop is structural — the underlying closing beyond the order block means the thesis failed. Fallbacks handle the moves that stall. All of them fail open: missing data never forces an exit.

Under the fill · market structure

What happens to an order after you hit send.

Sean's limit order doesn't go "to the market" — it travels a specific path through a specific structure. Knowing that structure is what turns order type, spread, and liquidity from trivia into edge.

01

Broker

The DAY limit lands at the broker (Alpaca), validated against buying power and routed — it never touches an exchange floor directly.

02

Routing

The broker sends it to a venue: one of ~18 options exchanges or a wholesaler that may offer price improvement inside the quote.

03

NBBO match

It must fill at or inside the National Best Bid & Offer. A market maker takes the other side; the limit price caps what you'll pay.

04

OCC clears

The Options Clearing Corporation becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer — guaranteeing the contract, erasing counterparty risk.

05

Settle

The position appears in the account. Options settle T+1; the OCC holds the obligation until you close, exercise, or let it expire.

§ The pieces of the market

U.S. listed options are one connected, standardized system — not a loose collection of dealers.

  • ~18 exchanges quote the same contracts, linked by the NBBO so no venue trades through a better price elsewhere.
  • The OCC is the sole clearinghouse — one guarantor behind every listed option in the country.
  • Market makers post two-sided quotes continuously; they're the counterparty when there's no natural seller.
  • Standard terms: each contract controls 100 shares, fixed strikes and expiries — a $2.50 premium is $250 of real capital.

$ Liquidity & the spread

Liquidity is the gap between the price you see and the price you get. Two numbers reveal it before you trade.

  • Open interest = contracts alive right now. Thin OI means a market maker is your only counterparty — and they set a wide price.
  • Bid/ask spread is a round-trip cost you pay on entry and exit. On a $2.50 option a $0.40 spread is 16% gone before the thesis plays.
  • The quote is not the fill. A limit at the mid asks for price improvement; a market order accepts whatever the book offers.

⌥ Order types that matter

The order type is a contract with the market about the worst price you'll accept.

  • Limit — names your max (buy) or min (sell). Sean uses these exclusively. You may not fill, but you never overpay.
  • Market — fills now at any price. On options this is how you donate the spread. Sean never uses one.
  • Stop — a resting trigger that becomes an order when price is hit. Sean's stops live in software, re-evaluated every 5-minute cycle.

⚠ The spread tax — a real lesson from the desk

Some names Sean scores well have option chains too illiquid to trade. An option quoted $3.10 / $3.90 has a 26% round-trip spread — the position must gain a quarter of its value just to break even on the plumbing. When the chain is that wide, the disciplined move is to trade the shares instead of the option, or pass. A great setup on an untradeable contract is not a trade.
